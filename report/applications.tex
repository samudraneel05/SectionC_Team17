\begin{abstract}
3D Field of Junctions (FoJ) is a training-free denoiser that fits each
patch of a volume with planar wedges meeting at a common vertex and
averages the overlapping fits. Its demonstrated applications are
low-dose X-ray CT, cryo-electron tomography, and point-cloud denoising.
We apply the authors' publicly released implementation to three domains
outside that set, porous-media micro-CT, an MRI phantom, and binary
porous media, and compare it with Gaussian, median, and total-variation
(TV) filtering under controlled noise levels. On micro-CT, classical
filters are stronger at moderate noise, but FoJ overtakes all of them
once the input falls below about $11$~dB PSNR, exceeding the best
classical result by $3.2$~dB at the highest noise tested while retaining
boundary structure the filters lose. The other two domains mark the
boundary of the method: it does not help where the structure is smooth
or smaller than the patch scale.
\end{abstract}

%=====================================================================
\section{Introduction}

Volumetric images acquired under dose and time limits are noisy, and
denoising is a routine step in their analysis. Classical denoisers
include local filters such as Gaussian smoothing and median filtering,
variational methods such as total-variation (TV)
minimization~\cite{rudin1992nonlinear,chambolle2004algorithm}, and
patch-recurrence methods such as non-local means~\cite{buades2005nonlocal}
and BM3D~\cite{dabov2007image}; learned denoisers trained on paired
data~\cite{zhang2017beyond} dominate at moderate noise levels.

Field of Junctions (FoJ)~\cite{verbin2021foj} is a training-free
structural prior that models each image patch as constant-intensity
regions separated by boundaries meeting at a vertex, called a junction.
3D FoJ~\cite{moeini2026foj3d} extends the model to volumes: three planes
meeting at a common vertex divide each cubic patch into constant-valued
wedges, and overlapping patch fits are averaged into a denoised volume.
The method targets the low-SNR regime and was demonstrated on low-dose
X-ray CT, cryo-electron tomography, and point-cloud denoising.

We apply the released 3D FoJ implementation to three domains outside
that set. Grayscale micro-CT of porous rock is a natural fit for the
wedge model, since grains and pores form near-constant regions with
sharp, piecewise-planar boundaries. An MRI phantom and two binary
porous-media volumes are poor fits: the first has smooth tissue
intensities, the second has structure smaller than the patch scale.
Testing all three under controlled noise shows where the method
transfers. Our implementation and experiment scripts are available in
the accompanying
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/samudraneel05/foj3d-factor-study}}

\section{Methodology}

FoJ partitions a volume into overlapping cubic patches of $R$ voxels per
side. Inside each patch, three planes through a common vertex split the
patch into wedge-shaped regions, and each region takes a single constant
intensity (Fig.~\ref{fig:junction}). A patch is fitted by searching over
plane orientations and vertex positions to minimize the squared error
against the input patch; each region's intensity is the mean of the
voxels it covers. Since every voxel belongs to several overlapping
patches, the denoised volume is the average of all patch fits covering
that voxel. The patch size $R$ is the method's main hyperparameter: it
sets the largest noise scale that averaging can absorb, and the smallest
structure a wedge model can represent.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ext/fig_junction_full.png}
\caption{Junction schematics reproduced from~\cite{moeini2026foj3d}
(CC~BY~4.0). Left: 2D junctions with three wedge regions. Right: 3D
junctions with two regions formed by planes through a common vertex.}
\label{fig:junction}
\end{figure}

Because each region is a constant, the model removes noise by replacing
every wedge of the volume with a regional mean. Two consequences follow.
Structure that is not piecewise-flat at the patch scale, such as smooth
texture or features smaller than $R$, cannot be represented and is
averaged away; and the wedge approximation is a coarse model, so it pays
off only when the noise it removes exceeds the bias it introduces.

We use the authors' released implementation at its default
configuration.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\paragraph{Setup.}
We test three domains with public ground truth: a $192^3$ crop of
Bentheim sandstone micro-CT ($5.85\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ voxels, grains tens
of voxels across~\cite{bentheim2015zenodo}) under additive Gaussian
noise at $\sigma\in\{15\%,30\%,40\%,55\%\}$ of the dynamic range; the
BrainWeb T1 phantom ($181{\times}217{\times}181$~\cite{collins1998brainweb})
under Rician noise at $\sigma\in\{3\%\ldots13\%\}$; and two binary
porous-media volumes (a synthetic sphere pack and a segmented Berea
scan~\cite{neumann2020sandstones}) with feature sizes of about
2~voxels, under Gaussian noise at $\sigma{=}15\%$. Baselines are
Gaussian filtering, a $3^3$ median filter, and
TV~\cite{rudin1992nonlinear,chambolle2004algorithm} at three weights,
all under identical conditions; FoJ runs at $R\in\{6,8,12\}$, stride 2.
We also report a boundary F-score over gradient-magnitude edge voxels,
since edge preservation is FoJ's distinguishing feature.

\paragraph{Porous-media micro-CT.}
Table~\ref{tab:bentheim} and Fig.~\ref{fig:curve} show a clean
crossover. FoJ trails at $\sigma\le30\%$, overtakes every classical
baseline between 30\% and 40\%, and its margin grows with noise. The
advantage is concentrated at boundaries: at $\sigma{=}55\%$ FoJ retains
boundary-F1 $=0.85$--$0.88$ against $0.54$ for the best TV weight, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:slices} shows the grain--pore interfaces preserved where
TV loses them. The best patch size grows with noise: $R{=}8$ wins at
$\sigma{=}55\%$.

\paragraph{The other two domains.}
On the BrainWeb
T1 phantom~\cite{collins1998brainweb} under Rician noise, FoJ trails a
median filter by $1$--$5\dB$ at every tested level: smooth tissue
intensities are not piecewise-flat, so the wedge model's bias outweighs
its denoising. On two binary porous-media volumes (a synthetic sphere
pack and a segmented Berea scan~\cite{neumann2020sandstones}) whose
features span about 2~voxels, FoJ's output collapses toward the mean
(Fig.~\ref{fig:collapse}), reaching $7.1\dB$ on a $19.5\dB$ input:
structures smaller than the patch cannot be represented by wedges.

$11\dB$; the control domains stay negative because their violation is
structural rather than noise-driven.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Across three volumetric domains outside the original study's set, 3D FoJ
succeeds on the one whose structure its wedge model can express at high
noise: porous-media micro-CT, where it outperforms Gaussian, median, and
TV denoising once the input falls below about $11\dB$ PSNR, with a
$+3.2\dB$ margin and sharply better boundary preservation at the highest
noise tested. Smooth MRI tissue and sub-patch-scale binary volumes fail.
The released implementation is an effective training-free denoiser for
low-SNR volumes with piecewise-flat structure; outside that structure,
classical filters remain the better choice.
